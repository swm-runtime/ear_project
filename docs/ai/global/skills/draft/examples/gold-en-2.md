### #1 A meal without an unpacked kitchen

[E-um] E1 · A delivered meal doesn't need the family's own kitchen. Jjajangmyeon is a Korean-style Chinese dish of noodles mixed with a shiny black sauce. It's known as an easy dish to have delivered, especially when people move into a new home. In Korea, many families hire a moving company that does the packing for them. In that kind of move, the old kitchen may stay in use until the crew arrives that morning.

[Grace] Y3 · How much of the packing does that crew do?

[E-um] E2 · The service is called pojang isa, or a packing move. The company can pack, transport, and arrange the household's belongings as one service. During one family's moves, a family member would start helping with a box and tape. A mover would take over because that help slowed the crew's routine. The family soon stood near the doorway holding phones and wallets. The workers packed the home.

[Grace] Y4 · Even the person with the tape ends up waiting by the door.

[E-um] E3 · The new home has a different problem. On another family's moving day, the dishes and pots weren't in place yet. Unopened boxes filled the rooms. Nobody could cook a home meal. For a long time, jjajangmyeon was closely tied to moving day in Korea. It came quickly and didn't need a ready kitchen.

### #2 How the noodles became an everyday delivery meal

[Grace] Y5 · Was it always easy to get?

[E-um] E4 · It was once a luxury that not everyone could get. Flour was in short supply in Korea until some years after the Korean War. Right after the war, large amounts of American wheat flour began arriving, and that marked the start of the dish reaching ordinary people. Even so, it remained a special-occasion food for some time. By the 1960s, more pig farming, more onions, and government policies that encouraged grains other than rice helped it become popular.

[Grace] Y6 · That's quite a change from a meal for a special occasion.

[E-um] E5 · In the 1980s and 1990s, it became known as a quick, cheap meal that filled people up. That happened especially as Chinese restaurants began to deliver it.

[Grace] Y7 · Could a family just order a bowl to the new home by then?

[E-um] E6 · Jjajangmyeon was already an established delivery meal. It is also common and affordable across Korea. A family whose new kitchen wasn't ready could still have it brought to the door.

### #3 Feeding the people who helped

[Grace] Y8 · Why did this particular meal become so closely tied to moving?

[E-um] E7 · It also fed the people who carried the furniture. Before professional moving services became familiar, a family might rent a small truck. They asked relatives and friends for help. Neighbors could help too. Belongings traveled in collected cardboard boxes and plastic basins. Blankets and tied bags also held things during the move.

[Grace] Y9 · One household's move takes up a lot of people's time.

[E-um] E8 · Friends and relatives gave their time and strength. That kind of help stayed familiar longer in smaller cities and the countryside than in Seoul. Once the furniture was inside, the helpers sat down for a meal. Jjajangmyeon fed people who had spent hours on that work.

[Grace] Y10 · Who provided the meal for everyone?

[E-um] E9 · The family thanked friends and relatives by paying for lunch or dinner. The bowls arrived while boxes still crowded the new home. People ate on the floor or around a table that had already been assembled. They shared the meal before the helpers went home.
